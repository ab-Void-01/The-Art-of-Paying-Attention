To Live Is to Keep Letting Go

ASWords by The VoidJul 06, 2026

I kept a shoebox of concert tickets for a decade before I admitted I was never going to look at them again. Letting them go felt, absurdly, like a small death. Which tells you something about how attached I get to things that were never really the point — the ticket was never the concert. The concert was already over the moment I walked out of the venue.

We're taught to hold on. Hold onto relationships, jobs, identities, versions of ourselves that made sense once and haven't since. Letting go gets framed as failure, as giving up, as a kind of quitting. Nobody tells you that a hand closed in a permanent fist can't actually hold anything new. It can only keep guarding what's already gone stale.

The leaf doesn't apologize

A tree doesn't grieve its leaves in autumn. It doesn't cling to last summer's growth out of loyalty. It lets go completely, without ceremony, trusting a process it can't see the end of — because holding on would cost it the spring. That's not loss. That's the mechanism of the thing continuing to be alive at all.

Everything I've ever had to let go of made room for something I couldn't have held onto the old thing and also received.

What I'm practicing

I'm not naturally good at this. I still keep boxes I should empty, in every sense of the word. But I'm learning to notice the difference between grief, which deserves its full time, and grip, which just delays the next thing from arriving. Not every ending needs to be fought. Some of them are just the tree doing, quietly and on schedule, exactly what it needs to do to bloom again.

So: an open hand, then, more often than a closed one. Not because nothing matters, but because everything that ever mattered to me arrived only after I stopped white-knuckling whatever came before it.

Let something go today, gently,
